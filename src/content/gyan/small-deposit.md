Twenty per cent is often treated as the magic number for a deposit. It's a useful target, but it isn't a rule, and waiting to reach it can mean years of rent while prices move.

## Why 20% matters to lenders

With a 20% deposit you're borrowing 80% of the property's value. Above that, the lender sees more risk, and usually asks you to pay **lenders mortgage insurance (LMI)**.

## What LMI is (and isn't)

LMI protects the **lender**, not you, if a loan goes bad and the property sells for less than what's owed. It's typically a one-off cost that rises as your deposit shrinks. It can often be added to the loan rather than paid upfront, though you'll then pay interest on it.

LMI isn't automatically a bad deal. Paying it can make sense if it gets you into the market years sooner.

## Ways to buy with less

- **Government schemes.** The federal Home Guarantee Scheme, run by Housing Australia, can let eligible buyers purchase with a deposit as small as 5% without paying LMI. Eligibility rules and property price caps apply and do change, so check the current details.
- **State support.** States run their own first home buyer grants and stamp duty concessions, which can reduce how much cash you need.
- **Family guarantee.** A family member uses equity in their property as extra security for your loan. It can remove the need for LMI, but it's a serious commitment for them, so everyone should get independent advice.
- **LMI waivers for some professions.** Some lenders waive or discount LMI for certain occupations.

## Things to weigh up

- A smaller deposit means a bigger loan and bigger repayments
- You'll have less equity if prices dip
- Keep a buffer after settlement, and don't empty every account to get in

## The takeaway

A smaller deposit can work well. What matters is that the repayments fit your life, and that you understand the costs. What applies to you depends on your situation, so ask before you assume.
