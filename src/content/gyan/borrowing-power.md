Two people on the same salary can get very different answers from a lender. That's because borrowing power is about what's left over, not just what comes in.

## Income

Lenders look at your base salary, plus how stable it is. Overtime, bonuses, commissions, rental income and self-employed income are often counted too, but sometimes only partly, or only with a longer track record.

## Living expenses

You'll be asked about your regular spending: groceries, transport, childcare, subscriptions, insurance and so on. Lenders compare what you declare with benchmarks and your bank statements, and generally use whichever figure is higher.

## Existing debts

Car loans, personal loans, HECS-HELP debts and buy now pay later accounts all reduce how much you can borrow, because the repayments come out of the same income.

## Credit card limits, even unused

This catches many people out. Lenders usually treat a card's full limit as if it could be spent, and count a monthly repayment on it. A $15,000 limit you never use can still reduce your borrowing power. Lowering or closing unused cards before you apply can help.

## Dependants

Children and other dependants increase expected living costs, which lowers borrowing capacity.

## The interest rate buffer

Lenders also check you could still afford repayments if rates rose. They test your loan at a rate a few percentage points higher than the actual rate. That buffer is why your borrowing power is often lower than a quick online calculator suggests.

## Ways to improve your position

- Pay down or close small debts
- Reduce credit card limits you don't need
- Keep spending steady and visible in the months before applying
- Make sure all your income is properly documented

Knowing what lenders look at helps you walk in prepared, and borrow an amount that still lets you live your life.
